\subsection{Macrostate distribution in log space}
The multiplicity $\Omega(N,k)=\binom{N}{k}=N!/[k!\,(N-k)!]$ grows like $2^N$, so it is computed through its logarithm with the log-Gamma function ($\Gamma(n+1)=n!$, \texttt{math.lgamma}):
\begin{equation}
\ln\Omega(N,k)=\ln\Gamma(N+1)-\ln\Gamma(k+1)-\ln\Gamma(N-k+1).
\end{equation}
\texttt{ln\_omega(N, k)} implements it and \texttt{ln\_omega\_array(N)} evaluates every $k=0\dots N$ at once with \texttt{scipy.special.gammaln}; both are tested against the exact \texttt{math.comb(N, k)} (Section~\ref{sec:methodology}).

\paragraph{(c) $N=20,50,80,100$.} Figure~\ref{fig:distribution} shows $\Omega(N,k)$ for the four sizes on a logarithmic axis, which is needed because the peaks span 24 orders of magnitude (Table~\ref{tab:peaks}). Each curve is symmetric about $k=N/2$, where the most probable macrostate sits, and falls to $\Omega=1$ at the ordered states $k=0$ and $k=N$. The values are exact integers, so they carry no uncertainty; the log-Gamma route reproduces them to a relative $10^{-11}$. Near the peak each curve is a Gaussian of width $\sigma=\sqrt N/2$: in absolute terms the peak widens with $N$, but relative to the system size it narrows as $\sigma/N=1/(2\sqrt N)$.

\paragraph{(d) Largest practical $N$.} Three successive limits, measured on an Apple M4 Max laptop with 48\,GB of RAM:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Floating-point range (the limit for $\Omega$ itself).} A float64 holds at most $1.8\times10^{308}=e^{709.78}$. The peak $\Omega(N,N/2)$ exceeds it first: \texttt{largest\_float\_N()} gives $N_{\max}=1029$, since $\ln\Omega(1030,515)=710.25$ (in the tests, \texttt{float(math.comb(1030, 515))} raises \texttt{OverflowError}). $2^N$ overflows at $N=1024$, so $\Omega/2^N$ must also be formed in log space. Beyond $N=1029$ only $\ln\Omega$ can be plotted.
\item \textbf{Exact integers.} \texttt{math.comb} never overflows, but $\Omega(N,N/2)$ has $\approx0.3N$ digits: a whole row took $0.66$\,s for $N=5000$, $4.4$\,s for $N=10^4$ and $30.6$\,s for $N=2\times10^4$ (time $\propto N^{2.8}$), reaching 5 minutes at $N\approx4.5\times10^4$.
\item \textbf{Log space.} $\ln\Omega\approx N\ln2$ never overflows; a row costs about $11$\,ns and $32$\,B per point ($1.1$\,s and $3.2$\,GB for $N=10^8$), so memory caps a full row at $N\approx1.5\times10^9$. Precision then takes over: $\ln\Omega$ is a difference of three numbers of size $\approx N\ln N$, so its absolute error is about one unit in the last place of $\ln\Gamma(N+1)$: compared with the Stirling/Gaussian form, $2\times10^{-7}$ at $N=10^8$, $2\times10^{-3}$ at $10^{12}$, $0.2$ at $10^{14}$ ($\Omega$ uncertain by $\approx20\%$) and $4$ at $10^{15}$. Past $2^{53}\approx9\times10^{15}$, $k$ itself cannot be stored exactly in a float.
\end{itemize}
\textbf{The limiting factor is the finite range and precision of floating-point numbers}: $\Omega$ can be computed and plotted up to $N=1029$, and $\ln\Omega$ up to $N\sim10^{13}$ (error below $0.01$). Plotting is a weaker constraint: for large $N$ only the $\sim\sqrt N$ values of $k$ around $N/2$ are visible, so $10^8$ points carry no more information than a thousand.

\paragraph{(e) Scaling to $N\sim10^{23}$.} By the de~Moivre--Laplace theorem, the binomial distribution tends to a Gaussian. Using Stirling's formula, $\ln n!\simeq n\ln n-n+\tfrac12\ln(2\pi n)$, around the peak:
\begin{equation}
\ln\Omega(N,k)\simeq N\ln2+\tfrac12\ln\frac{2}{\pi N}-\frac{2\,(k-N/2)^2}{N},
\end{equation}
i.e.\ the Gaussian of Eq.~\eqref{eq:gaussian_limit} with $\sigma=\sqrt N/2$. This form (\texttt{gaussian\_ln\_omega}) differs from the exact value at the peak by only $1/(4N)$ in $\ln\Omega$: $0.0125$ for $N=20$ ($1.3\,\%$ in $\Omega$) and $0.0025$ for $N=100$. For $N=10^{23}$:
\begin{itemize}
\item the peak sits at $k=5\times10^{22}$ with $\ln\Omega_{\max}\simeq N\ln2=6.93\times10^{22}$, i.e.\ $\Omega_{\max}\approx10^{3.01\times10^{22}}$, yet it holds only a fraction $P(N/2)\simeq\sqrt{2/(\pi N)}=2.5\times10^{-12}$ of the $2^N$ microstates; essentially all of them lie within a few $\sigma$ of the peak (99.99\,\% within $4\sigma$);
\item the width $\sigma=\sqrt N/2=1.6\times10^{11}$ coins is enormous in absolute terms but only $\sigma/N=1.6\times10^{-12}$ of the system: against $k/N$ the distribution is a Dirac delta at $1/2$;
\item a macroscopic deviation is impossible in practice: an imbalance of one part per million, $k=\tfrac N2(1\pm10^{-6})$, is suppressed by $e^{-2(k-N/2)^2/N}=e^{-5\times10^{10}}\approx10^{-2.2\times10^{10}}$.
\end{itemize}
This is why thermodynamics works: the fraction of heads is sharply $1/2$, fluctuations scale as $1/\sqrt N$ and become unmeasurable, and $S=k_B\ln\Omega_{\max}\simeq Nk_B\ln2$ is extensive. Neither $\Omega$ nor \texttt{lgamma} can be used at this size (one unit in the last place of $\ln\Gamma(10^{23}+1)$ is $\approx10^9$), so the large-$N$ results come from the analytic expressions above (Part~2\,k).
