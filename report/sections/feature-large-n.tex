\subsection{The Second Law and the large-$N$ limit}

\paragraph{(j) Do any of these results contradict the Second Law?} No. Several of them show the entropy \emph{decreasing}: in (e) the macrostate $k=85$ has $10.1\,k_B$ less entropy than the peak, and a toss can perfectly well land there. But the Second Law, read statistically, does not forbid entropy decreases; it states that an isolated system overwhelmingly evolves towards, and stays in, the macrostate with the most microstates, because every microstate is equally likely. A macrostate with entropy $\Delta S$ below the maximum is visited with relative probability $e^{\Delta S/k_B}$, which is never zero. For $N=121$ this is easy to observe: re-tossing the coins once per second, a macrostate at least as far from the peak as (e) ($k\le36$ or $k\ge85$) has probability $9.81\times10^{-6}$ and appears on average once every $1.02\times10^{5}$\,s $\approx28$ hours. These are fluctuations, and they are large precisely because the system is small. Started from an ordered macrostate such as $k=20$, re-tossing takes the system to the peak and raises the entropy by $29.3\,k_B$ (question (g)): this is the Second Law at work, not against it. The same law that allows the fluctuations makes them disappear for macroscopic $N$, as (k) shows: the probability of finding the fraction of heads more than 1\% away from $1/2$ is $0.856$ for $N=121$ and $0.047$ for $N=10^4$ (exact sums), but only $10^{-8.7\times10^{18}}$ for $N=10^{23}$. At that size an observed entropy decrease is not forbidden, it is just never going to be seen; the Second Law becomes, in practice, deterministic.

\paragraph{(k) The limit $N\sim10^{23}$.} The exact log-Gamma route of the previous sections cannot be used here: one unit in the last place of $\ln\Gamma(10^{23}+1)$ is $\approx10^9$, and $k=N/2+25$ is not even representable as a float. Every answer is therefore re-derived analytically and computed in terms of offsets $d=k-N/2$ (\texttt{solve\_part2\_k}):
\begin{itemize}
\item small $k\ll N$: $\ln\binom{N}{k}=\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\ln(N-i)-\ln k!\simeq k\ln N-\ln k!-\frac{k(k-1)}{2N}$;
\item near the peak (Stirling, see Part~1\,e): \[P(N/2)\simeq\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi N}},\qquad \frac{\Delta S_{k_1k_2}}{k_B}\simeq-\frac{2(d_1^2-d_2^2)}{N};\]
\item intervals: $P(d_1\le k-N/2\le d_2)\simeq\frac12\bigl[\operatorname{erf}\frac{d_2+1/2}{\sigma\sqrt2}-\operatorname{erf}\frac{d_1-1/2}{\sigma\sqrt2}\bigr]$, with $\sigma=\sqrt N/2=1.58\times10^{11}$.
\end{itemize}
The tests check each approximation against the exact value at $N=10^4$--$10^6$ (relative differences between $10^{-6}$ and $10^{-3}$, shrinking as $N$ grows). Table~\ref{tab:largeN} compares the answers.

The table splits into two opposite behaviours, and both are the same statement: what matters is the distance from the peak \emph{relative to} $\sigma\propto\sqrt N$.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Fixed offsets become invisible.} (b) The most probable macrostate becomes itself improbable, $P\propto N^{-1/2}\to0$, because the probability spreads over $\sim\sigma$ neighbours. (c, d) A window of $\pm10$ or $\pm20$ coins, which held $93\%$ and $99.98\%$ of the states at $N=121$, now holds $\simeq(2w+1)\,P(N/2)\sim10^{-10}$: it is $10^{10}$ times narrower than $\sigma$. (e, f) Moving 10 or 25 coins away from the peak costs no entropy ($\sim10^{-20}\,k_B$), and (i) in the $N/2\pm10$ reading those macrostates are as likely as the peak to 21 decimal places. Physically these states are indistinguishable from the peak.
\item \textbf{Fixed fractions become impossible.} (a, h, i) A macrostate with a few heads out of $10^{23}$ has $P\simeq2^{-N}$, i.e.\ $\log_{10}P\simeq-N\log_{10}2=-3.0103\times10^{22}$ (the $30\ln N$ and $\ln k!$ corrections are far below the float resolution of that number); waiting times and likelihood ratios are the inverse, $10^{3\times10^{22}}$, beyond any physical meaning. (g) The entropy difference between the ordered state and the peak becomes macroscopic, $\Delta S\simeq Nk_B\ln2=0.957$\,J/K, the entropy of mixing of one-sixth of a mole of two-state systems.
\end{itemize}
For (j), the conclusion of the previous paragraph becomes exact in the limit: the relative width $\sigma/N=1/(2\sqrt N)=1.6\times10^{-12}$ vanishes, the fraction of heads is sharply $1/2$, and any macroscopic entropy decrease has a probability $e^{-\mathcal O(N)}$. The statistical Second Law turns into the deterministic law of thermodynamics.
